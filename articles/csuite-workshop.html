The C-Suite Workshop Has to Inform the Platform Config

Yashika BarhaiyaAuthor
Aug 2026Published
4 min readLength

Planning transformation programmes follow a predictable structure: strategy workshops with senior leadership, then handoff to the implementation team, then platform configuration. The gap between those two phases is where most transformation value gets lost.

The strategy work surfaces how the business wants to make decisions — what trade-offs it's willing to make between service level and inventory, how it wants to handle demand uncertainty across markets, where it places responsibility for supply decisions. The implementation work translates requirements into platform configuration. The problem is that these two bodies of knowledge rarely meet in a way that produces a configured system that actually reflects the strategic intent.

The gap between what the C-suite agreed to in the workshop and what gets built in the platform is where transformation value disappears.

How the disconnect happens

It's not a failure of communication or documentation. It's structural. The people running executive alignment workshops are typically strategy consultants. The people configuring o9 or any other planning platform are technical implementers. In most programme structures, they're different people, often from different firms, working in sequenced rather than integrated ways.

The strategy team produces a target operating model and a set of design principles. The technical team receives those documents and interprets them into configuration decisions. The interpretation gap is where the problem lives.

A design principle like "prioritize service level for our top-50 customers" sounds clear. Translating it into planning constraint configuration requires a set of decisions that the design principle doesn't answer:

  • How is "top-50" defined and maintained in the master data?
  • What is the service level target, and how is it measured in the planning system?
  • When inventory is constrained, how does the system trade off between top-50 customers and others?
  • Who reviews and overrides these allocations, and what is their decision authority?

Each of these requires an answer to configure correctly. Without the strategy team present, the implementation team makes reasonable assumptions — and those assumptions may or may not reflect what the executives actually meant.

What changes when the same person runs both

The most effective configuration work I've done has been on engagements where I was in the executive workshop and in the configuration workstream — where I held both bodies of knowledge simultaneously, without a handoff in between.

What changes is the specificity of the strategic intent that reaches the configuration layer. When I'm in the room where the CSCO says "we need to be able to rebalance inventory across our top markets within a two-week window," I can ask the follow-up questions that a strategy deck won't record: what's the actual constraint — transport lead time, supplier flexibility, or something organizational? Are there markets that are always protected, regardless of global inventory position? What's the governance process for a rebalancing decision above a certain value threshold?

Those answers aren't in the workshop output. They're in the conversation. And they're the things that determine whether the planning system is configured to support the decision process the executives described, or a technically valid approximation of it that breaks down in the edge cases that matter most.

The luxury context makes this more important, not less

In luxury and beauty supply chains, the edge cases are more frequent and the decisions are more consequential. A rebalancing decision between Paris and Tokyo during a peak launch window isn't a routine operational call — it's a brand and commercial decision that requires judgment the system needs to support, not replace.

The executives in the workshop understand this intuitively. They know which decisions they want the system to escalate and which they want it to handle autonomously. Translating that understanding into planning exception logic, decision thresholds, and escalation workflows requires knowing what they said and what they meant — which is not always the same thing in the transcript.

A standard programme structure, where strategy and implementation run sequentially with a handoff, doesn't give the implementation team access to that distinction. They get the transcript version.

What this means for programme design

The practical implication is that planning transformation programmes need people who can hold both the strategic and technical dimensions simultaneously — not as a bridge between two separate workstreams, but as integrated work. The workshop insights need to be in the room when the configuration decisions are being made.

That's not always possible with large programme structures. But it's worth designing for. The alternative is a system that's technically configured correctly against requirements that don't fully capture what the business decided — and a gap that only becomes visible when the first planning cycle runs and the outputs don't reflect what the leadership team expected.

Running C-suite workshops and platform configuration as separate, sequenced activities is operationally convenient. It's not the right structure for delivering transformation that holds. The workshop has to inform the config — and that means the people doing the config need access to what actually happened in the workshop, not just what was written down afterwards.
